Why you sleep through your alarm (and 7 fixes that work)

7 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • You usually sleep through an alarm because it rings during deep sleep, when your brain needs much more noise to wake up.
  • Too little sleep makes deep sleep heavier, so it's the biggest single cause.
  • Your brain also tunes out a sound it hears every morning.
  • The fixes: sleep enough, wake at the same time daily, change and raise your alarm, put it across the room, add light, and make your brain do something before the alarm stops.

If you've ever slept straight through an alarm that woke up the whole house, you're not lazy and your phone probably isn't broken. There's a real, measurable reason some mornings feel impossible. Once you understand it, you can stack the odds in your favour.

What's actually happening

1. Your alarm rang during deep sleep

Sleep runs in cycles of roughly 90 to 110 minutes, moving through light sleep, deep (slow-wave) sleep and REM sleep. Sleep researchers use the term arousal threshold for how much sound or other stimulus it takes to wake you. That threshold is highest in deep sleep. An alarm that would wake you instantly from light sleep can go unnoticed if it lands in the middle of a deep stretch. That's why you hear your alarm on some days but not on others.

2. You're short on sleep

When you're sleep deprived, your brain catches up by making deep sleep more intense, and the threshold goes up with it. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours. If you're regularly getting 6, your alarm is fighting your biology.

3. Your brain has learned to ignore the sound

Brains are very good at filtering out sounds they hear all the time and that don't seem to mean danger. Hear the same chime every morning for a year and it can stop registering, especially if you've often switched it off without really waking up.

4. Your body clock says it's still night

If you're naturally a night owl, or you went to bed much later than usual, a 6 am alarm can land in what your body still treats as the middle of the night. Waking up then is genuinely harder.

5. Something else is deepening your sleep

Alcohol, sedating medications (some antihistamines and sleep aids, for example) and sleep disorders such as sleep apnea can all make waking harder. If you suspect this, talk to a doctor or pharmacist. Don't stop a prescribed medication on your own.

7 fixes that work

Fix 1: Protect your sleep first

This is the least exciting fix and the most effective one. Work backwards from your wake-up time: for a 6:30 alarm and an 8-hour target, you need to be asleep around 10:30, which means in bed a little before. Every other fix on this list works better when you're not running on a sleep debt.

Fix 2: Wake up at the same time every day

A steady wake time, weekends included, trains your body clock so that you're already rising toward light sleep when the alarm rings. Keep weekends within about an hour of weekdays. A "sleep-in" of three hours on Saturday feels great, but it makes Monday morning harder.

Fix 3: Plan your bedtime from your wake time

Getting enough total sleep matters most: adults need at least 7 hours. Some people also find it helps to aim for the end of a roughly 90-minute sleep cycle rather than the middle of one. A simple rule of thumb: count back in 90-minute blocks from your wake time, plus about 15 minutes to fall asleep. For a 6:30 alarm, that points to falling asleep around 11:00 pm (5 cycles, 7.5 hours) or 9:30 pm (6 cycles, 9 hours). Cycle lengths vary from person to person and night to night, so treat this as a rough guide, not a rule (more on how accurate it is).

Fix 4: Change your alarm sound, and make it build

To beat habituation, switch to a sound you haven't heard before, and change it every few weeks. Sounds that get louder, or that change pitch and rhythm, are harder to tune out than one repeating beep. Make sure the volume is actually high. Fix 5: Put the phone across the room

If you have to stand up to turn the alarm off, you're already halfway out of bed. Charge your phone on a dresser or by the door. It also keeps you from scrolling in bed, which helps with Fix 1.

Fix 6: Let light do some of the work

Light is the strongest signal to your body clock. Open the curtains as soon as you're up, or step outside for a few minutes in the first hour after waking. In winter, a bright lamp helps. Morning light also tends to make it easier to fall asleep at night, so it compounds.

Fix 7: Make your brain do something before the alarm stops

Many people "sleep through" alarms they actually switched off, half-awake, with no memory of doing it. A wake-up task that needs real attention, such as solving a short math problem, closes that gap. By the time you've solved it, you're awake enough to remember making a decision.

When to talk to a doctor

See a doctor if you regularly sleep through alarms even after 7 to 9 hours of sleep, if you snore loudly or wake up gasping, if you're very sleepy during the day, or if this started suddenly. These can be signs of a sleep disorder that's very treatable.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I sleep through my alarm but wake up for other sounds?

Your brain filters sounds by how familiar and how meaningful they are. A sound you hear every morning becomes "safe" background, while an unusual noise or someone saying your name gets through. Changing your alarm sound helps.

Do multiple alarms help?

Several alarms over 20 to 30 minutes can help some people wake gradually, but if you're switching each one off without waking, they're just interrupting your sleep. Fewer alarms that you can't dismiss half-asleep usually work better.

Is it bad to wake up from deep sleep?

It's not harmful, but it makes the groggy feeling after waking (sleep inertia) stronger. That grogginess usually fades within 30 to 60 minutes.
