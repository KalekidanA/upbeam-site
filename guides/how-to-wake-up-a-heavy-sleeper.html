How to wake up a heavy sleeper (kids, teens, partners and yourself)

7 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • A voice beats a beep. In sleep-lab studies of children, voice alarms woke 85–91% of kids, while a standard high-pitched tone woke only 53–56%.
  • Stack gentle signals: their name, light from the curtains, a smell from the kitchen, then a reason to stand up.
  • Timing matters. Deep sleep is the hardest stage to wake from. A regular bedtime and enough sleep make every method work better.
  • If someone sleeps 7+ hours and still can't wake, or falls straight back asleep, it's worth talking to a doctor.

Some people wake when a floorboard creaks. Others sleep through three alarms, a barking dog and someone calling their name. If you're the one trying to get a heavy sleeper out of bed every morning (a child, a teenager, a partner, a roommate), this guide covers what the research says works, what to try first, and when it's more than just a deep sleeper.

Why some people are so hard to wake

Sleep researchers talk about an "arousal threshold": how much noise or touch it takes to wake you. Heavy sleepers have a higher threshold than light sleepers. The Sleep Foundation notes that researchers still don't fully know why, but a few things clearly play a part:

  • Sleep stage. Your night runs in cycles of roughly 90 minutes. The lightest stage is the easiest time to wake someone; deep sleep is the hardest.
  • How long they've been awake. The longer someone has gone without sleep, the more deeply they sleep. A short night makes the next morning harder.
  • Brain rhythms. In a small 2010 study at Massachusetts General Hospital, people whose brains made more "sleep spindles" (short bursts of brain activity during sleep) were less likely to be woken by noise. Each person's spindle rate stayed fairly steady from night to night.
  • Age. Children are much harder to wake with sound than adults, and people tend to become lighter sleepers after about 60.
  • Sleep loss and health. Not getting enough sleep, sleep apnea, idiopathic hypersomnia, depression and some chronic conditions can all make mornings much harder.

What the alarm research found

The clearest evidence comes from smoke-alarm studies by Nationwide Children's Hospital in Ohio, which tested what actually wakes sleeping children aged 5 to 12 in a sleep lab.

  • In a 2018 study of 176 children, alarms using the mother's recorded voice woke 86–91% of them. A typical high-pitched tone alarm woke 53%.
  • The children woken by a voice got out of the room in a median of 18 to 28 seconds. With the tone, it took nearly five minutes.
  • A 2020 study of 188 children found a stranger's voice, male or female, worked about as well as a parent's. A low-pitched tone mixed with a voice did too.
  • Adding the child's first name to the message made no real difference.
  • In a related study, 149 of 150 adults aged 20 to 49 woke to all four alarms tested, usually within a second or two.

The takeaway for everyday mornings: for a deep sleeper, especially a child, a spoken voice tends to get through where a high, repeated beep doesn't. And if adults in the house wake easily, don't assume the kids will hear the same alarm.

Fire safety: the same researchers recommend a smoke alarm on every level of the home, one outside every sleeping area and, for the best protection, one inside each bedroom. When you replace alarms, consider a voice or low-frequency type.

How to wake a heavy sleeper, step by step

Start gentle and add signals one at a time. Most heavy sleepers respond to a combination, not one loud blast.

  1. Open the curtains or switch on the light. Daylight stops the body making melatonin, the hormone that keeps you sleepy. On dark winter mornings, a wake-up light or smart bulb that brightens on a schedule can do the same job.
  2. Use your voice and their name. Speak in a normal, calm voice from beside the bed rather than shouting from the doorway. Keep talking for a minute; one "time to get up" is easy to sleep through.
  3. Add a light touch. A hand on the shoulder or a gentle shake of the foot. Keep it gentle rather than startling: the aim is a calm wake-up, not a fright.
  4. Use smell and sound from elsewhere. Leave the bedroom door open while coffee brews or breakfast cooks, or play music they like. A 2020 study found people felt less groggy waking to melodic sounds than to a standard alarm tone.
  5. Get them vertical. Ask them to sit up and answer a question, or hand them a glass of water, so they have to move and talk.
  6. Come back in five minutes. It's easy to mumble "I'm up" and drift off again. A quick second check saves a late start.

If the heavy sleeper is you

You can't call your own name, but you can set things up so your alarm does the work:

  • Put the alarm across the room. It has to be loud enough to hear, and you have to stand up to stop it. Then don't climb back in.
  • Make it think. Healthline suggests alarms that ask you to solve a puzzle or a math problem before they stop, to get your brain working.
  • Check your alarm volume. If your iPhone alarm seems quiet, our guide to fixing a low iPhone alarm volume walks through the settings.
  • Get up at the same time every day. A regular schedule, weekends included, is one of the most consistent tips across the Sleep Foundation's advice.
  • Get more sleep. Most adults need at least 7 hours. Extra minutes of snoozing in the morning don't make up for a short night.

When it might be more than heavy sleeping

Being hard to wake is often about sleep stage, sleep loss or just how someone is built. But talk to a doctor if you notice:

  • Trouble waking even after a full night's sleep, or after naps.
  • Waking up and quickly falling back asleep, or strong sleepiness all day.
  • Loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing at night.
  • Mornings that keep getting in the way of school, work or driving.

These can be signs of conditions like sleep apnea, narcolepsy or idiopathic hypersomnia, which a doctor can diagnose and treat. Keeping a sleep diary for a week or two (bedtime, wake time, how you felt) gives a doctor a useful starting point.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to wake up a heavy sleeper?

Use a voice rather than a beep, say their name, open the curtains, and give them a reason to sit up. In studies of children, voice alarms woke far more kids than a standard high-pitched tone.

Why are some people such heavy sleepers?

Researchers don't fully know. Deep sleep makes anyone harder to wake, and sleep loss, genetics, age and some sleep disorders all play a part. People whose brains make more sleep spindles also tend to sleep through more noise.

Is it safe to wake someone who is sleepwalking?

Healthline says the idea that it's dangerous is not true. Gently guide them back to bed first, without much touching. If that doesn't work, make a loud noise from a safe distance.
