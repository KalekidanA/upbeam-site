How to wake up early when you're not a morning person

7 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • Being a night owl is partly built in, but your body clock can still move. In one study, night owls shifted about two hours earlier in three weeks.
  • Move your alarm and bedtime 15 minutes earlier, hold it for a few days, then move again. Don't jump an hour overnight.
  • The biggest levers: bright light right after waking, dim light at night, the same wake time every day (weekends too), early meals and no late caffeine.

If you've ever set an alarm for 6:00, felt like a zombie for a week and drifted back to your old hours, the problem probably wasn't willpower. You were fighting your body clock instead of moving it. The good news is that the clock can be moved, as long as you do it gradually and give it the right signals.

Why early mornings feel so hard for some people

Everyone has a chronotype: a natural lean toward earlier or later sleep. According to the Sleep Foundation, chronotype is largely genetic, but age, environment and activity level all influence it. Night owls tend to feel sleepy late and alert late, so a 6:00 alarm can land in what is, for their body, the middle of the night.

That's why simply setting an earlier alarm rarely sticks. If your body still thinks it's 2:00 a.m., you won't fall asleep at 10:00 p.m., you'll be short on sleep, and the alarm becomes something to fight. The goal is to shift the clock itself, so that the earlier time starts to feel normal.

What the research says: night owls can shift about two hours

In a 2019 study published in Sleep Medicine, researchers from the Universities of Birmingham, Surrey and Monash worked with 22 healthy night owls whose usual bedtime was around 2:30 a.m. and wake time around 10:15 a.m. For three weeks, they followed a few simple rules:

  • Wake up 2 to 3 hours earlier than usual and get as much outdoor light as possible in the morning.
  • Go to bed 2 to 3 hours earlier and limit light in the evening.
  • Keep the same sleep and wake times on work days and days off.
  • Eat breakfast soon after waking, eat lunch at the same time each day, and skip dinner after 7 p.m.

On average, their sleep and wake times moved about two hours earlier, with no loss of total sleep. They also reported less daytime sleepiness, stress and low mood, and their morning reaction times improved. It was a small, short study, and the researchers noted that long-term effects weren't yet known, but it shows the clock is more flexible than many night owls assume.

The plan: 15 minutes at a time

The study participants jumped straight to their new times. For most people with jobs and families, a gentler ramp is easier to keep. The Sleep Foundation recommends moving your alarm and bedtime about 15 minutes earlier, and waiting at least a few days before the next step.

Step 1: Pick your target and your starting point

Write down the time you actually wake up on a free day without an alarm. That's roughly where your clock sits now. Then pick a realistic target. Most adults need at least 7 hours of sleep, so a 6:30 wake-up means being asleep by about 11:30 at the latest.

Step 2: Move both ends by 15 minutes

Set your alarm 15 minutes earlier and plan to be in bed 15 minutes earlier too. Moving only the alarm just steals sleep. Keep this new time for three to four days, then shift again. If a step feels rough, stay on it longer rather than pushing ahead.

Step 3: Get bright light within minutes of waking

Morning light is the strongest signal that tells your body clock it's daytime, and it's what pulls your sleepy time earlier the following night. Step outside for a few minutes if you can, even on a cloudy day: outdoor light is far brighter than indoor lighting. If it's still dark when you get up, sit near the brightest window you have, or ask your doctor about a light therapy lamp.

Step 4: Make evenings dim

Light at night does the opposite and pushes your clock later. In the last hour or two before bed, switch to lamps instead of ceiling lights, lower screen brightness and, if you can, put the phone down. Blackout curtains help if streetlights or early summer sunrises wake you at the wrong time.

Step 5: Use meals and caffeine as signals

  • Eat breakfast soon after waking and keep lunch at a regular time. Regular meals are another cue for your body clock.
  • Eat dinner earlier where you can. In the study above, participants stopped eating after 7 p.m.
  • Keep caffeine to the morning. Caffeine can disturb sleep even when you have it six hours before bed, so a mid-afternoon coffee can quietly undo your earlier bedtime.

Step 6: Keep weekends close

This is where most attempts fall apart. Sleeping until 11:00 on Saturday moves your clock right back, and Monday feels like jet lag. Try to keep your weekend wake time within an hour of your weekday time. If you're short on sleep, a short early-afternoon nap is a better fix than a long lie-in.

Getting out of bed on the hard mornings

Even with a shifted clock, some mornings will be tough, especially in the first two weeks. A few things help:

  • Put the alarm out of reach so stopping it means standing up.
  • Prepare the first five minutes the night before: water by the bed, clothes ready, curtains easy to open.
  • Give mornings a reason. Something you look forward to, like a good coffee, a walk or 20 quiet minutes before anyone else is up, does more than guilt.
  • Expect grogginess. The foggy first half hour is called sleep inertia. It's normal and fades, so judge the new schedule after you're up and moving, not in the first minute.

When to get help

If you're sleeping 7 hours or more and still can't wake up, are very sleepy during the day, or you simply can't fall asleep before the early hours no matter what you try, talk to a doctor or sleep specialist. Conditions like sleep apnea, insomnia and delayed sleep-wake phase disorder are common and treatable. And if your life allows it, working with a later schedule is a perfectly good choice too.

Frequently asked questions

Can a night owl really become a morning person?

Partly. You probably won't turn into someone who loves 5:00 a.m., because chronotype is largely genetic. But studies show night owls can move their sleep and wake times about two hours earlier with light, consistent timing and regular meals, and feel better for it.

How long does it take to adjust to waking up earlier?

At 15 minutes every three or four days, moving 90 minutes takes about three weeks. The 2019 study saw a two-hour shift in three weeks with a stricter routine. Consistency matters more than speed.

Should I take melatonin to fall asleep earlier?

Some sleep doctors suggest a small dose a few hours before bedtime to help shift the clock earlier, but timing and dose matter, and it isn't right for everyone. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before trying it, and start with light and schedule changes first.
