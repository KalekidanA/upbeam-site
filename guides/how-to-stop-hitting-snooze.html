How to stop hitting snooze: a 14-day plan

8 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • Snoozing is very common, and research suggests a short snooze isn't harmful for people who are used to it.
  • The real cost is that you trade solid sleep for broken, light sleep, and your mornings start late and rushed.
  • The plan: 3 days measuring, 4 days resetting your alarm and making getting up easy, 7 days locking it in.

Most of us know the routine: the alarm rings, a hand reaches out, and nine minutes later it rings again. Then again. You're not alone. In a 2023 survey of 1,732 adults by researchers at Stockholm University, about 69% said they snooze at least sometimes. The most common reason was simply feeling too tired to get up.

First, is snoozing actually bad?

You may have read that snoozing "ruins your whole day." The evidence is more relaxed than that. In the same study, regular snoozers spent nights in a sleep lab and were allowed either to snooze for 30 minutes or to get up at the first alarm. Snoozing didn't hurt their mood, their sleepiness or their sleep the rest of the night, and they even did slightly better on some thinking tests right after waking. The researchers were careful to say this applied to habitual snoozers who fall back asleep easily, and that snoozing isn't for everyone.

So why stop? Because snoozing still costs you sleep. The time between alarms is lighter, broken sleep. If you snooze for 30 minutes, you could have had 30 minutes of uninterrupted sleep by setting the alarm later. For many people, snoozing also means starting every day late and in a rush, which is the real problem.

If you like a gentle wake-up, there's a middle path: one deliberate snooze, planned into your schedule, instead of five unplanned ones. The plan below works either way.

The 14-day plan

Days 1–3: Measure, don't change anything

Keep your routine exactly as it is and write down four things each morning: when you fell asleep (roughly), when the first alarm rang, how many times you snoozed, and when your feet actually touched the floor. Three days is enough to see your real pattern. Most people find a gap of 20 to 40 minutes between "alarm" and "up."

Day 4: Reset your alarm to your real get-up time

Take the average time you actually got up over the last three days. That's your new alarm time. Not earlier. You're keeping the same wake time you already have, but turning broken sleep into solid sleep. Then turn off snooze for that alarm, or limit yourself to one.

On iPhone, you can turn off Snooze when editing an alarm in the Clock app, and since iOS 26 you can also change how long a snooze lasts.

Days 4–7: Make getting up the easy option

  • Phone across the room. If you have to stand up to stop the alarm, snoozing takes effort and getting up doesn't.
  • Light, right away. Open the curtains or switch on a bright light within a minute of standing up. Light is the strongest "it's daytime" signal for your body clock.
  • Prepare one first action the night before. A glass of water by the alarm, clothes on the chair, or the coffee maker ready. A small, specific first step beats willpower.
  • Make the alarm need your attention. An alarm that asks you to solve a quick problem before it stops makes it much harder to dismiss it on autopilot.

Days 8–10: Fix the real cause, which is usually bedtime

If you still feel wrecked at the alarm, you probably need more sleep, not more discipline. Move your bedtime 15 minutes earlier every two days until mornings feel manageable. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours. A useful check: if you set an alarm for 6:30 and want about 7.5 hours (five 90-minute sleep cycles), aim to be asleep by 11:00.

Days 11–14: Lock it in

  • Keep weekends within an hour of your weekday wake time. Big weekend lie-ins shift your body clock and make Monday feel like jet lag.
  • Count your streak. Mark each morning you get up at the first alarm. Seeing a run of good mornings is a quiet but real motivator.
  • Review. Compare days 1–3 with days 11–14: how many minutes of "snooze time" did you get back as real sleep?

If it still isn't working

If you're getting 7 to 9 hours and still can't get up, or you're very sleepy during the day, it's worth talking to a doctor. Conditions like sleep apnea, depression and some medications can all make mornings much harder, and they're treatable. You may also simply be a strong night owl. In that case, a later schedule, where you have the choice, is a fair solution, not a failure.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a snooze be?

There's no proven ideal. If you do snooze, keep it short and planned, and count it in your total sleep. A single 10-minute snooze you planned for is very different from 40 minutes of repeated alarms.

Why am I so groggy even when I don't snooze?

That's sleep inertia: the slow, foggy feeling after waking. It usually fades within 30 to 60 minutes and is stronger when you're short on sleep. Light, movement and water help.

Can I change the snooze time on my iPhone?

Yes. Since iOS 26 the Clock app lets you choose the snooze length instead of the old fixed 9 minutes. Since iOS 26.1 you also slide to stop an alarm, while snooze is still a tap.
