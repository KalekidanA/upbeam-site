Sleep cycle calculator: what time should I go to bed?

7 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • The formula: wake-up time − (number of cycles × 90 minutes) − 15 minutes to fall asleep = when to get into bed.
  • Aim for 5 cycles (7.5 hours) or 6 cycles (9 hours). Four cycles is only 6 hours, below the 7 hours adults need.
  • Cycles vary, so treat the answer as a starting point. If you wake before the alarm and feel fine, your timing is working.

You know when you have to be up. The question is when to go to bed so the alarm doesn't catch you at the worst possible moment. Sleep cycle calculators answer that by counting back in 90-minute blocks. The idea is sound enough to be useful, as long as you know where it stops being precise. Below you'll find the ready-made table, the formula to work out any time yourself, and how to tune it to your own body.

Going to bed now and want to know when to set the alarm? Run it forward instead: bedtime + 15 minutes + cycles × 90 minutes. Lights out at 11:00 p.m. gives a wake time of 6:45 a.m. for five cycles or 8:15 a.m. for six.

How to work out your own bedtime

  1. Start with the wake time you can't move. Work, school or the kids decide this one, so build backwards from it.
  2. Pick your number of cycles. Five cycles (7.5 hours) suits most adults. Choose six (9 hours) if you're catching up on sleep, or you know you need more.
  3. Multiply by 90 minutes and subtract. Five cycles is 7 hours 30 minutes; six is 9 hours.
  4. Subtract the time it takes you to fall asleep. The Sleep Foundation puts normal at about 10 to 20 minutes for a healthy adult, which is why 15 is a sensible default. If you usually lie awake for half an hour, use 30.
  5. Set a reminder 30 to 60 minutes earlier so you have time to wind down before that bedtime arrives.

Example: you need to be up at 6:45 a.m. and you fall asleep in about 20 minutes. Five cycles back from 6:45 is 11:15 p.m., minus 20 minutes, so lights out at 10:55 p.m.

What a sleep cycle actually is

Through the night, your brain moves through a repeating loop of stages. According to the Sleep Foundation, a typical night has 4 to 6 of these cycles, averaging about 90 minutes each:

  • N1: the drowsy, lightest stage as you drift off, lasting just a few minutes.
  • N2: light sleep. Your body temperature drops and your heart rate slows. It makes up about half of the night.
  • N3: deep, slow-wave sleep. This is the hardest stage to wake from.
  • REM: the dreaming stage, with an active brain. It's about a quarter of adult sleep.

The mix changes as the night goes on. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half, while REM periods get longer towards morning. That's why an alarm at 3 a.m. feels brutal and one near your natural wake time feels gentler: later in the night you spend more time in lighter stages.

Being woken from deep sleep is one reason for that heavy, groggy feeling called sleep inertia. Timing your alarm to land near the end of a cycle is an attempt to avoid it.

How accurate is a sleep cycle calculator?

Roughly accurate, not precisely. It helps to know why:

  • Cycles aren't exactly 90 minutes. The Sleep Foundation describes a first cycle of 70 to 100 minutes and later ones of 90 to 120. Australia's Sleep Health Foundation notes that cycle length differs between people and can change from night to night for the same person.
  • Falling asleep takes a different time each night. Stress, caffeine or going to bed before your body clock is ready can all stretch it out.
  • Brief night wakings are normal and push everything later in ways no calculator can predict.
  • Age, alcohol and recent sleep change the pattern, so last week's timing may not fit tonight.

Sleep experts interviewed by Popular Science in 2025 made the same point: there's no reliable way to make your cycles run to an exact 90 minutes. So don't treat a calculator as a precise prescription. Its real value is that it makes you plan a bedtime that gives you enough sleep, and that part is well supported.

What matters more than hitting a cycle

Enough total sleep

The CDC recommends at least 7 hours a night for adults, and in its 2022 survey data between 30% and 46% of adults, depending on the state, got less than that. Five full cycles gets you there. If you're choosing between "4 cycles on the dot" and "about 7.5 hours", always pick the longer night.

The same wake time every day

A steady wake time, weekends included, is what anchors your body clock. Once it's anchored, you'll tend to get sleepy at a similar time each night and often surface naturally just before your alarm. That's the best sign your timing is right. Our guide on how to wake up early when you're not a morning person shows how to shift that anchor gradually.

Light in the morning, dim light at night

Bright light soon after waking and dimmer screens in the last hour before bed help your body clock match the bedtime you've chosen, so you actually fall asleep in those 15 minutes.

Tune it to your own cycles in a week

  1. Use the table for 7 nights with the same wake time.
  2. Each morning, rate how you felt in the first 10 minutes: groggy, okay or good.
  3. If you're often groggy, move your bedtime 15 minutes earlier or later and try again for a few nights. Small changes like this can make up for cycles that run a little longer or shorter than 90 minutes.
  4. Keep the bedtime that gives you the most "good" mornings.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I go to bed if I wake up at 6 a.m.?

For five cycles (7.5 hours) plus about 15 minutes to fall asleep, get into bed around 10:15 p.m. For six cycles (9 hours), aim for 8:45 p.m.

Is it better to sleep 6 hours or 7.5 hours?

7.5 hours. Six hours lines up neatly with four cycles, but it's below the 7 hours or more the CDC recommends for adults. More total sleep beats perfect timing.

Are sleep cycle calculators accurate?

Only roughly. Cycle length and how fast you fall asleep vary from night to night, so use the result as a starting point and adjust it by 15 minutes based on how you feel in the morning.
