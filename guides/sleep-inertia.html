Sleep inertia: why you feel groggy after waking (and how to shake it)

7 min read · Updated November 2026

The short version
  • Sleep inertia is the foggy, slow, clumsy feeling right after you wake up. It's normal, and it isn't a sleep disorder.
  • It usually lasts 15 to 60 minutes, and longer when you're short on sleep or wake from deep sleep.
  • What helps most: enough sleep, a steady wake time, caffeine in the morning, short naps, and giving yourself a buffer before anything important.

The alarm goes off, you sit up, and your brain feels like it's still loading. You read the same text message three times. You put the milk in the cupboard. That heavy, half-awake state has a name: sleep inertia. It's the gap between your body being awake and your brain fully catching up, and almost everyone has it to some degree.

The good news is that it always passes. The better news is that you can make it shorter and milder.

What sleep inertia actually is

Sleep inertia is a short period of grogginess, disorientation and reduced thinking speed right after waking. Reaction time, short-term memory, decision-making and learning are all a little worse while it lasts. That's why sleep researchers suggest giving yourself extra time before anything that really matters, like driving or a big decision, straight after waking.

Scientists don't yet know its exact cause. The main ideas are that parts of the brain stay in a "sleep-like" pattern for a while after you open your eyes, that a sleepiness chemical called adenosine is still high when you wake, and that blood flow to the brain takes time to return to its daytime level. Brain scans show some features of sleep linger after waking, which fits the first idea.

How long does it last?

For most people, the worst of it is over within 15 to 60 minutes. A 2019 review of the research found that performance often gets back to its pre-sleep level within 15 to 30 minutes, but full recovery takes at least an hour, and some kinds of thinking can stay slower for a couple of hours or more.

It lasts longer when you've been short on sleep. In one study the review describes, people who got about 5.6 hours of sleep a night did roughly 10% worse on a test right after waking, and were still below their normal level 70 minutes later.

What makes morning grogginess worse

  • Not enough sleep. Sleep debt is the biggest one. When you're very short on sleep, your brain reaches deep sleep sooner, and the grogginess after waking can last longer.
  • Waking from deep sleep. Waking from deep (slow-wave) sleep tends to feel worse than waking from light sleep. CDC guidance for nurses notes that you typically reach deep sleep about an hour after falling asleep, so being woken around then can leave you especially foggy.
  • Waking at your body clock's low point. Grogginess is stronger when you wake near the bottom of your daily rhythm, in the early hours of the morning, when your core body temperature is lowest. This is why very early alarms and night shifts hit so hard.
  • An irregular schedule. Night-shift workers, teenagers and people with a lot of sleep debt tend to have stronger sleep inertia. Repeated snoozing may also bring on fresh bouts of it, because you keep drifting back into sleep and waking again.
  • Long naps. Naps longer than about 30 minutes are more likely to leave you groggy afterwards.
  • Alcohol, smoking and some medications can also make waking harder.

How to shake it off faster

1. Get enough sleep (the fix that matters most)

Because sleep loss makes inertia both stronger and longer, the most effective fix is unglamorous: more sleep. If you're always groggy, try moving your bedtime 15 minutes earlier every few nights and see whether mornings get easier.

It helps to see, every night, how much sleep your alarm actually leaves you. Upbeam shows "rings in X h Y m" on every alarm, so at bedtime you can tell at a glance whether you're heading for 6 hours or 8.

2. Keep the same wake time every day

A regular bedtime and wake time keeps your body clock in step, so your alarm goes off when your body already expects to be waking. Keep weekend wake-ups close to your weekday time if you can.

3. Have caffeine in the morning

Caffeine blocks adenosine, which is one reason it's among the better-supported ways to shorten sleep inertia. A coffee or tea soon after waking can help. Don't overdo it, and keep it to the earlier part of the day so it doesn't spoil the next night's sleep.

4. Get light, and keep your room dark at night

Bright light after waking helps you recover from a long, heavy bout of grogginess, though studies haven't found it does much for short, mild ones. It's still a good habit for your body clock: daylight in the morning, darkness at night. Blackout curtains help keep the night dark.

5. Keep naps short

If you nap, keep it short so you wake before deep sleep. Advice ranges from 10 to 15 minutes (UCLA Health) to under 20 minutes (CDC guidance for workers) to no more than 30 minutes (Sleep Foundation). Set an alarm. One study in the 2019 review found 10-minute naps gave an immediate boost, while 30-minute naps took a while to pay off because of the grogginess afterwards.

6. Choose a gentler alarm sound and a cool, quiet room

The Sleep Foundation suggests a gentle alarm sound rather than a jarring one, and a cool bedroom with a quiet fan. Research on sounds and music for sleep inertia is still limited and mixed, so treat this as comfort rather than a cure.

7. Build in a buffer

Since your brain needs time to catch up anyway, plan for it. Give yourself 20 to 30 minutes of easy routine (shower, breakfast, getting dressed) before driving, important messages or hard decisions.

When to talk to a doctor

Morning grogginess that clears within an hour is normal. If it doesn't lift, keeps getting in the way of your day, or you still feel very sleepy after a full night in bed, it's worth seeing a doctor. Keeping a simple sleep diary for a couple of weeks (bedtime, wake time, how you felt) makes that conversation easier, and a doctor may suggest a sleep study to look for an underlying cause.

Frequently asked questions

How long does sleep inertia last?

Usually 15 to 60 minutes. Much of the slowness clears in the first 15 to 30 minutes, but research suggests full recovery takes at least an hour, and after short sleep it can last a few hours.

Why do I feel groggy even after a full night's sleep?

Sleep inertia happens to well-rested people too. It's stronger if your alarm wakes you from deep sleep, if you wake at an unusual time for your body clock, or if you've been short on sleep for several nights.

Does coffee help with sleep inertia?

Yes. Caffeine is one of the better-supported countermeasures, because it blocks adenosine, a chemical linked to sleepiness. Keep it to the morning so it doesn't disturb the next night's sleep.
